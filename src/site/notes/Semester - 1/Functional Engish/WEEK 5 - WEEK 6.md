# WEEK 5

## Levels of Reading Comprehension

### **Literal Reading**

- **Definition:** Understanding the explicit, plain facts directly stated in the text (_reading on the lines_).
    
- **Focus:** Identifying main ideas, factual details, characters, settings, and sequence of events.
    
- **Key Question:** _"What does the text directly say?"_
    

### **Inferential Reading**

- **Definition:** Drawing logical conclusions by combining text clues with prior knowledge (_reading between the lines_).
    
- **Focus:** Understanding implied meanings, cause-and-effect relationships, tone, and underlying motives.
    
- **Key Question:** _"What does the author imply or mean?"_
    

### **Critical Reading**

- **Definition:** Analyzing and evaluating the validity, bias, logic, and effectiveness of the text (_reading beyond the lines_).
    
- **Focus:** Assessing evidence quality, identifying bias or assumptions, evaluating arguments, and questioning author intent.
    
- **Key Question:** _"Is the author's argument sound, logical, and persuasive?"_
    

## Paragraph Writing

> [!NOTE]
> A standard academic paragraph contains **three main structural parts**:
> 
> - **Topic Sentence:** The opening sentence that states the main idea and controlling thought of the paragraph.
>     
> - **Supporting Sentences:** Middle sentences that provide evidence, examples, details, explanations, or statistics to prove the topic sentence.
>     
> - **Concluding Sentence:** The final sentence that summarizes the key point, provides a closing thought, or bridges to the next paragraph.
>     

## Four Major Types of Essays

|**Essay Type**|**Primary Goal**|**Key Characteristics**|
|---|---|---|
|**Expository**|To explain, inform, or educate on a topic objectively.|Uses facts, statistics, and logical structure without personal opinion.|
|**Persuasive / Argumentative**|To convince the reader to agree with a specific viewpoint or take action.|Uses logical reasoning, evidence, counterarguments, and call to action.|
|**Descriptive**|To paint a vivid picture of a person, place, object, or event.|Rich in sensory details (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell) and figurative language.|
|**Narrative**|To tell a real or fictional story.|Follows a chronological plot structure (exposition, climax, resolution) with characters and dialogue.|

## Cohesion and Coherence

> [!NOTE]
> 
> - **Cohesion (Grammatical Linkage):**
>     
>     - The **mechanical/linguistic connection** between words and sentences.
>         
>     - _Achieved through:_ Pronouns, repetitive keywords, transitional phrases, and grammatical agreement.
>         
> - **Coherence (Logical Flow):**
>     
>     - The **conceptual and logical unity** of the overall text.
>         
>     - _Achieved through:_ Logical ordering of ideas (chronological, spatial, order of importance) and clear relationship between paragraphs.
>         

## Transition Words (Categories & Examples)

> [!NOTE]
> Transition words build bridges between sentences and paragraphs to improve reading flow:
> 
> - **Addition:** _Furthermore, moreover, in addition, also, besides._
>     
> - **Contrast / Opposition:** _However, on the other hand, nevertheless, conversely, yet, in contrast._
>     
> - **Cause & Effect / Result:** _Therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence._
>     
> - **Example / Illustration:** _For instance, specifically, to illustrate, for example._
>     
> - **Emphasis:** _Indeed, in fact, notably, especially, crucially._
>     
> - **Conclusion / Summary:** _In conclusion, overall, to summarize, ultimately, in short._

## Thesis Statement: Comprehensive Guide

> [!NOTE]
> A **thesis statement** is a 1–2 sentence declaration that states the main argument, central claim, or core purpose of an essay or academic paper. It acts as a roadmap for the reader and establishes the scope and focus of the entire text.
> 
> ### Core Components of a Strong Thesis Statement
> 
> Every effective thesis statement combines three key elements:
> 
> $$\text{Thesis Statement} = \text{Topic} + \text{Specific Claim} + \text{Supporting Reasons / Roadmap}$$
> 
> 1. **Topic**: The primary subject matter under discussion.
>     
> 2. **Claim**: Your specific stance, assertion, or argument regarding the topic.
>     
> 3. **Reasoning / Scope**: The key evidence or sub-arguments used to justify the claim.

### Key Characteristics of an Effective Thesis

> [!NOTE]
> - **Debatable**: It must present a point of view that reasonable people could agree or disagree with, not an undisputed fact.
>     
> - **Focused & Specific**: It covers a manageable aspect of a broader topic rather than making overly broad generalizations.
>     
> - **Clear & Direct**: It avoids vague, ambiguous language or conversational expressions (e.g., "In my opinion," "I think").
>     
> - **Arguable & Supportable**: It can be backed up with evidence, facts, and logical reasoning throughout the body paragraphs.

### Types of Thesis Statements

|**Type**|**Purpose**|**Example Structure**|**Example**|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Argumentative**|Takes a clear stance on a controversial topic and aims to persuade the reader.|State claim + list specific arguments supporting the claim.|_Public transit funding should be doubled because it reduces urban carbon emissions, eases traffic congestion, and provides equal economic mobility for low-income residents._|
|**Expository / Informative**|Explains, clarifies, or educates the reader on a topic without taking a biased stance.|State topic + break down the key sub-topics to be explained.|_The rapid expansion of online remote work is driven by advances in cloud collaboration software, shifting corporate priorities toward reduced overhead, and employee demand for flexible scheduling._|
|**Analytical**|Breaks down an issue or idea into its component parts to evaluate how they interact.|State subject + present analysis of key mechanics or thematic components.|_An analysis of Shakespeare’s Macbeth reveals that the protagonist’s downfall is caused by his unbridled ambition, his wife’s manipulation, and his fatalistic trust in prophecy._|

### Strong vs. Weak Thesis Examples

- **Weak (Too broad)**: _Social media is bad for teenager mental health._
    
- **Strong**: _Excessive social media consumption among teenagers contributes to elevated rates of anxiety and depression by promoting unrealistic social comparisons, disrupting sleep patterns, and replacing face-to-face social interaction._
    

- **Weak (Statement of fact)**: _The American Civil War occurred between 1861 and 1865._
    
- **Strong**: _While economic disparities between the North and South contributed to tension, the fundamental cause of the American Civil War was the ideological divide over the expansion of slavery into new territories._
    

### How to Draft a Thesis Statement in 4 Steps

> [!NOTE]
> 1. **Start with a research question**: _What impact does remote learning have on elementary students?_
>     
> 2. **Formulate an initial answer (working thesis)**: _Remote learning negatively affects elementary students._
>     
> 3. **Add evidence and scope**: _Remote learning negatively affects elementary students by decreasing social engagement, increasing screen fatigue, and exacerbating educational inequality._
>     
> 4. **Refine and polish**: _Although remote learning offers schedule flexibility, it ultimately harms elementary education by reducing peer socialization, increasing screen fatigue, and widening the achievement gap among socioeconomically disadvantaged students._

    

## 2. Précis Writing and Precise Writing

### What is a Précis?

A **précis** (pronounced _pray-see_) is a clear, concise, objective summary of a text that retains the original author's main arguments, tone, logical structure, and overall message in a fraction of the original length (typically **one-third** of the original word count).

### Core Rules of Précis Writing

> [!NOTE]
> 
> - **Length**: Strict target length—usually $25\%$ to $33\%$ of the original passage's total word count.
>     
> - **Voice & Person**: Always written in the **third person** ("The author argues...", "The passage asserts...") and in **indirect speech**.
>     
> - **Objectivity**: Must remain strictly neutral. Do **not** include personal opinions, critiques, or external facts not present in the original text.
>     
> - **Original Words**: Use your own language and sentence structure; avoid direct quotations or lifting phrases from the original text.
>     
> - **Sequence**: Maintain the author's logical progression of ideas and central flow.
>     
> - **Omissions**: Omit all non-essential elements: illustrations, historical anecdotes, quotes, rhetorical questions, statistics, and decorative adjectives.
>     
> 

### Step-by-Step Method for Writing a Précis

```
[ Read Passage ] ──> [ Identify Main Idea & Sections ] ──> [ Omit Examples & Fluff ]
                                                                   │
[ Polish & Check Word Count ] <── [ Draft in Third Person ] <──────┘
```

> [!NOTE]
> 1. **First Reading**: Read the passage carefully to grasp the general meaning and core theme.
>     
> 2. **Second Reading**: Identify key supporting points, main arguments, and section shifts. Underline keywords.
>     
> 3. **Outline & Calculate Target Word Count**: Count the total words in the original passage and divide by 3 to set your target word limit.
>     
> 4. **Draft the Précis**: Combine the main points into coherent paragraphs using smooth transitional words (e.g., _furthermore, consequently, however_).
>     
> 5. **Title the Précis**: Provide a brief, suitable title that encapsulates the main topic.
>     
> 6. **Edit for Conciseness**: Remove redundant words, replace long phrases with single precise words, and verify word count compliance.

### Principles of Writing Precisely (General Conciseness)

Writing precisely means expressing ideas with maximum clarity using the fewest, most effective words possible.

#### 1. Eliminate Wordy Phrases

|**Wordy Phrase**|**Precise Alternative**|
|---|---|
|_Due to the fact that_|**Because**|
|_At this point in time_|**Now** / **Currently**|
|_In spite of the fact that_|**Although** / **Despite**|
|_In the event that_|**If**|
|_Conducted an investigation of_|**Investigated**|
|_Has the capability of_|**Can**|

> [!NOTE]
> #### 2. Replace Weak Verbs + Adverbs with Strong Verbs
> 
> - **Wordy**: _She walked very quickly across the room to loudly express her disapproval._
>     
> - **Precise**: _She dashed across the room to denounce the decision._
>     
> 
> #### 3. Shift from Passive to Active Voice
> 
> - **Passive (Wordy)**: _The survey was conducted by the research team, and the data was analyzed by the software._
>     
> - **Active (Precise)**: _The research team conducted the survey and analyzed the data using software._
>     
> 
> #### 4. Avoid Nominalizations (Turning Verbs into Nouns)
> 
> - **Wordy**: _The committee came to a conclusion that an implementation of the new policy was necessary._
>     
> - **Precise**: _The committee concluded that the new policy was necessary._
